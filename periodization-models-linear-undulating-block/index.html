Linear vs Undulating vs Block Periodization: Which Model Actually Builds Better Athletes

By Coach Daniel Webb

Photo: Victor Freitas / Pexels

Most coaches spend years debating periodization like it's a religion. You're either a linear guy, a daily undulating devotee, or you've gone all-in on block periodization after reading too much Issurin. I get it. I've been there. But after 14 years working with professional and collegiate athletes across three sports, I've come to believe the debate itself is the problem. Coaches argue models when they should be arguing fit. The right periodization structure isn't the most theoretically elegant one. It's the one your athlete can actually recover from, adapt to, and peak with on the date that matters.

My position is clear: block periodization, when properly sequenced, produces the most reliable performance peaks for advanced athletes with defined competition calendars. But that doesn't make it universally superior. And pretending linear periodization is outdated does a disservice to the thousands of developing athletes who still respond beautifully to it. What I want to do here is cut through the noise, give you the honest tradeoffs, and help you make a better decision for your specific situation.

Linear Periodization Still Works, Just Not for Everyone

Linear periodization is the original model. Volume starts high, intensity climbs progressively, and you taper into a competition or test date. It's predictable, it's easy to teach to younger coaches, and it works remarkably well for novice to intermediate athletes. The neuromuscular system responds strongly to progressive overload when it hasn't been exposed to much of it yet. So if you're training high school athletes or early-stage collegiate lifters, don't abandon this model just because it's been around since the Soviet era.

The honest limitation is detraining of qualities you're not currently emphasizing. If you spend 8 weeks in hypertrophy ranges, your maximal strength expression tends to dip. Spend 6 weeks peaking for strength, and work capacity suffers. This quality-versus-quality tradeoff is acceptable in short training blocks, but it becomes a real problem across a full season, especially in sports where you need strength, speed, and aerobic capacity simultaneously. That's where linear periodization starts to crack.

I've seen this pattern play out with football linemen who peaked beautifully in October, only to look flat and slow by December playoffs. The model worked until it didn't. That's not a failure of the athlete. It's a structural mismatch between the model and the sport's demands.

Daily Undulating Periodization Isn't a Magic Fix, But It's Close for Some Populations

Daily undulating periodization, commonly called DUP, flips the script by rotating training qualities within the same week or even the same training day. Monday might be strength focused at 85 to 90 percent of one-rep max. Wednesday shifts to hypertrophy ranges, around 70 to 75 percent. Friday becomes a power or speed-strength session. The idea is that more frequent variation in stimulus keeps adaptation rates higher without the detraining effect you see in strictly sequential models.

A 2002 study by Rhea and colleagues found that DUP produced significantly greater strength gains compared to traditional linear periodization over a 12-week period in resistance-trained men. That finding gets cited constantly, and for good reason. But it gets overcited without context. The study used trained men, not elite athletes with 6 to 8 years of structured training. Extrapolating those results to a professional sprinter or an Olympic weightlifter involves some real leaps in logic.

Where DUP genuinely shines is with general fitness athletes, CrossFit-style trainees, and team sport players who need to maintain multiple physical qualities across a long, undulating season with no clean off-season block. But in my experience, the more advanced the athlete, the more they need concentrated stress to drive adaptation, and that's where DUP starts to lose ground to block models.

Why Block Periodization Wins for Advanced Athletes With Clear Peak Dates

Block periodization, developed largely through the work of Vladimir Issurin and popularized in his 2008 book on training theory, organizes training into sequential mesocycles called accumulation, transmutation, and realization. Each block has a concentrated training focus, and each block builds on the qualities developed in the last. You accumulate general physical capacity first, convert it into sport-specific strength and power second, and then realize it in competition-specific performance. It's sequential, but intentionally so.

What separates this approach from classic linear periodization is the residual training effect concept. Issurin's model accounts for how long different physical qualities remain elevated after training ceases. Aerobic endurance residuals last around 25 to 30 days. Maximal strength holds for 20 to 30 days. Speed and power qualities decay fastest, often within 5 to 7 days. Knowing this lets you sequence blocks so that earlier qualities are still partially active when you layer the next training emphasis on top. That's not magic. It's physiology applied intelligently.

In my work with a professional rugby sevens program a few seasons back, we ran a 16-week block structure into the international tournament window. Accumulation ran 5 weeks, transmutation ran 6 weeks, realization ran 3 weeks, with a short 2-week taper. The athletes hit peak sprint speed and peak strength outputs within the same 10-day window before competition. That kind of synchronization is genuinely hard to produce with either linear or DUP models.

The Real Variable No Model Can Account For: Athlete Readiness

Here's the common misconception I run into constantly, even among experienced coaches: they treat periodization models as fixed programs rather than frameworks. Block periodization isn't a spreadsheet you hand an athlete in week one and execute blindly for 16 weeks. No periodization model is. The model tells you the general direction of training stress. Readiness monitoring tells you how hard to push on any given day or week. Ignoring the latter because you're committed to the former is a fast track to overtraining or underperformance.

When I tested heart rate variability monitoring alongside block periodization with a small group of collegiate throwers, the athletes who adjusted load based on daily HRV trends outperformed those following the fixed block prescription by a statistically meaningful margin across a single 12-week cycle. And the injury rate in the HRV-adjusted group dropped by nearly half. The model gave us the framework. Readiness data gave us the precision. You need both.

The honest area of debate in the strength and conditioning field right now is how to handle athletes who compete year-round or near year-round, the tennis players, the golfers, the MMA fighters. Block models were built around defined preparation and competition periods. When competition never really stops, traditional block structures get messy. Some coaches use mini-blocks of 3 to 4 weeks. Others use a modified concurrent approach. There's no clean consensus, and anyone who tells you otherwise is selling something.

If you're coaching a developing athlete or working with someone new to structured training, start with linear periodization and don't apologize for it. If you're managing a large team with diverse needs and a chaotic schedule, DUP gives you flexibility without complete quality loss. But if you're preparing an advanced athlete for a specific competition date 12 to 20 weeks out, build a block structure, sequence your mesocycles around residual training effects, and layer in readiness monitoring to keep the prescription honest. Pick up your calendar right now, identify your athlete's next major performance date, count back 16 weeks, and sketch your three blocks. That's where good periodization planning actually starts.
