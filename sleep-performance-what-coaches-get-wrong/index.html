Your Athletes Aren't Overtrained, They're Underslept

Sleep as a performance enhancer is real, but most coaches measure the wrong thing. What actually moves the needle for rest and recovery.

DW
Daniel Webb July 15, 2026 · 3 min read

If your athlete keeps stalling on the same numbers, check their sleep before you touch their program. The biggest single-week jump I've ever produced in a college 200m sprinter didn't come from a block change or a new lift. It came from moving his lights-out from 1am to 11:30pm for eleven days straight, and watching his flying-30 times drop by about a tenth. He thought I'd given him something. I'd just given him sleep.

Here's my problem with how most coaches talk about rest. We treat it like the absence of work, a passive gap between sessions where nothing happens. That's backwards. Sleep is when the adaptation you paid for in training actually gets built, and if you shortchange it you're leaving strength, speed, and healthy tissue on the table. Most of the mistakes I see aren't about training too hard. They're about resting badly and calling it recovery.

Recovery Days Are Not Sleep, And Coaches Confuse The Two

A day off is not the same as a good night. I've watched athletes take a full rest day, spend it gaming until 2am, then wonder why they feel flat on Monday. The training load came down. The sleep debt didn't. Those are separate accounts and you can't pay one with the other.

The research on this is blunt. When Cheri Mah's team at Stanford extended basketball players to around ten hours in bed for five to seven weeks, their sprint times, free-throw accuracy, and three-point shooting all improved, and reaction speed climbed too. That study, published in SLEEP in 2011, is old news now, but I still meet coaches who've never adjusted a single thing because of it.

Rest days manage fatigue. Sleep drives adaptation. If your program has one and ignores the other, you're doing half the job and taking credit for the whole thing.

The Injury Angle Nobody Puts On The Whiteboard

This is the part that should scare you more than a bad set of numbers. Chronic short sleep doesn't just slow athletes down. It hurts them.

A study of adolescent athletes led by Matthew Milewski found that kids sleeping fewer than eight hours a night were around 1.7 times more likely to get injured than those sleeping eight or more. You can read the summary of that 2014 injury-risk research and then try to justify the 11pm film sessions and the 6am lifts stacked on the same athlete. I can't.

In my work with youth and semi-pro groups, the ones who kept getting soft-tissue tweaks almost always had the worst sleep hygiene. Late screens, irregular bedtimes, a training schedule that left barely seven hours in the tank. We fixed the schedule before we changed a single exercise, and the tweaks mostly stopped. Correlation, sure. But I'll take the trade every time.

Your Wearable Is Measuring The Wrong Thing

So many coaches have handed the sleep problem to a device and stopped thinking. A recovery score turns green, everyone relaxes. But a number on a wrist doesn't tell you whether the athlete actually got enough hours, or just efficient hours in a too-short window.

I like the data. I've used it for years, and the arguments around it are real, as anyone who followed the Whoop 5.0 debate among strength coaches in 2024 knows. The trap is treating the score as the goal instead of a rough gauge. A device is good at spotting trends across weeks. It's mediocre at telling one athlete on one morning that they're fine to hit max effort.

The other issue is what we choose to track. We got obsessed with load and output because GPS and accelerometers made it easy, and the way Catapult's 2022 data push reshaped how coaches use GPS tracking is a big part of that. We can tell you a player covered 6,200 meters at high intensity. We rarely ask if they slept enough to absorb it. If you want the fuller argument on where this measurement culture is heading, the 2024 wearable reckoning for strength coaches lays it out well.

What I Actually Change, And Where It Falls Apart

Enough theory. Here's what I put in front of athletes, in plain terms.

  • Anchor the wake time first, not the bedtime. A consistent alarm drags the whole rhythm into line faster than nagging about lights-out.
  • Protect the two hours before bed. No hard conditioning, no screens jammed against the face, no caffeine after mid-afternoon. Around 8pm is my cutoff for most.
  • Bank the hours, don't chase deep-sleep percentages. Total time in bed is the lever that actually moves. The stage breakdown is mostly noise you can't control anyway.

And here's the thing... it doesn't always work. I had a shift-working amateur boxer, worked nights, and no amount of sleep gospel from me changed his roster. My friend who coaches rugby tried the same wake-time trick with a squad of students and honestly half of them just moved their gaming later and lied about it, which was equal parts funny and depressing. I'm not 100 percent sure the strict protocols land for everyone, especially adults with jobs and kids. Naps helped the boxer more than any bedtime rule I could invent. So I stopped preaching one system and started asking what their actual life allowed.

The misconception I fight most is that more sleep makes you soft or lazy. It's the opposite. The athletes who sleep most are usually the ones who can train hardest without breaking.

Tonight, pick one athlete and set a fixed wake time, then count backward to give them at least eight hours in bed. Don't add a supplement, don't buy a gadget, don't change the program. Run it for two weeks and time something you already measure. If sleep is the enhancer I think it is, the number will tell you before the athlete does.

  • Milewski et al., Chronic Lack of Sleep and Athletic Injuries (2014)

Frequently asked questions

How many hours should an athlete actually sleep?

Most competitive athletes do best with 8 to 10 hours a night, more than the 7 hours the CDC lists as a general adult minimum. Training raises the recovery demand, so the harder the load, the more time in bed you need to absorb it.

Does one bad night of sleep really hurt performance?

A single short night mostly dents reaction time, focus, and perceived effort rather than raw strength. The real damage comes from stacking short nights across weeks, which is when speed drops and injury risk climbs.

Can naps make up for lost sleep at night?

Naps help, especially a 20 to 30 minute window in the early afternoon, and they genuinely blunt the cost of a short night. But they supplement nighttime sleep, they don't replace the deeper restorative hours you only get in a full night.
