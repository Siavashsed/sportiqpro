**Title:** Does anyone else come out of a deload feeling weaker? I think most of us lighten the wrong variable

I keep seeing the same deload in programs, including ones I wrote years ago. Sets, reps and load all drop for the week, and then the first heavy session back feels slow and flat.

As a coach, I've had better results treating volume and intensity separately. Cut volume hard, about 40 to 50 percent fewer working sets. Keep your top sets at about 85 to 90 percent of what you've been handling. Fatigue still comes down because total work drops. You're still handling heavy weight, so the bar doesn't feel strange when you ramp back up.

I learned this the hard way in 2019 with a pro rugby forward. He took a full week of light everything and needed a while to get his numbers back. Since I switched approaches, the week after a deload usually feels sharp instead of rusty.

How do you all run deloads? Do you drop intensity, volume or both? Has anyone tracked RPE or bar speed coming out of each version? I'd like to hear what's worked over a longer stretch, not just one cycle.

A deload is meant to clear fatigue. Fatigue comes mostly from total volume, not from the weight of your heaviest sets. Yet the usual deload drops everything at once: sets, reps and load.

## QUORA
**Answer this question:** How do you deload without losing strength?

Cut the volume hard and leave most of the load on the bar. Most strength lost during a deload comes from making the week too easy, not from the rest itself.

Why that works: the fatigue you're trying to clear builds up mostly from total work, meaning the sets and reps across the week. The heavy weight itself does far less of the damage. Your ability to express strength depends on regularly handling heavy loads. If you drop the weight to 50 or 60 percent for a full week, heavy weight starts to feel unfamiliar. The first session back then feels slow and awkward, even though you're less tired.

Here's what I use with athletes:

1. Cut working sets by roughly 40 to 50 percent. If you normally do 5 working sets on a lift, do 2 or 3.
2. Keep top sets at about 85 to 90 percent of what you've been handling recently. Don't chase PRs, but don't drop to empty-bar territory either.
3. Keep reps per set at or a bit below normal, and stop well short of failure.
4. Keep accessory work light and short. It's the easiest place to remove fatigue without losing anything that matters.

I learned this in 2019 with a professional rugby forward who took a full week of light training across the board. He came back flat, and we spent time rebuilding strength he should never have lost. Since then I've programmed deloads this way, and athletes usually come out feeling sharp rather than rusty.

One caveat: if you're deloading because of an injury or because you're badly run down, that's a different situation. There, cutting intensity too can make sense.
