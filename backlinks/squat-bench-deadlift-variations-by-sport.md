## QUORA
**Answer this question:** How should athletes choose squat, bench and deadlift variations for their specific sport?

Short answer: choose the variation that matches your sport's joint angles, body positions and force demands, not the one that lets you lift the most weight.

I learned this the hard way in 2019. I had a collegiate soccer midfielder back squatting 405 for five reps. It looked great in the log, but his 30-meter sprint didn't improve. The lift was impressive and largely irrelevant to what he needed on the pitch.

Here is the framework I use now.

1. Start with the sport. Where do forces get produced? Is it a horizontal drive, a vertical jump, a rotational throw, or a static brace against contact?

2. Match positions. A front squat or safety bar squat keeps the torso more upright and shifts load toward the quads and upper back. A box squat or low-bar squat biases the hips. A split squat trains single-leg force, which matters for running and cutting.

3. Match the bench to the job. Grip width, arch and range of motion change which muscles and joints take the load. A contact-sport athlete may benefit from pressing variations that suit shoulder health and pushing mechanics rather than a powerlifting-style setup.

4. Treat the classic lifts as a starting point. They are useful for building general strength, but most athletes eventually need variations that carry over more directly.

5. Respect the athlete. Training age, injury history and mobility all change what is appropriate.

None of this means abandoning the big three. It means being clear about what each variation is buying you.
