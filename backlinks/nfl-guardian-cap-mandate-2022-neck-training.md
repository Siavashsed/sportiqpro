## QUORA
**Answer this question:** Do Guardian Caps make neck strengthening unnecessary for football players?

No, and I'd push back on anyone who says they do. I'm a strength and conditioning coach, and this question has come up a lot since the NFL's 2022 mandate.

Some context first. In 2022 the NFL required Guardian Caps, which are soft padded shells worn over the helmet, for offensive and defensive linemen, tight ends and linebackers during preseason practices. The league reported that concussions in those position groups dropped by roughly half compared with previous seasons. That's a real result, and I support the caps.

But the cap and neck training do different jobs.

A Guardian Cap adds padding at the point of impact, so it takes some of the force out of helmet-to-helmet and helmet-to-ground contact. It does nothing about how the head moves when the body takes the hit. A blindside shot to the chest can whip the head around without the helmet touching anything.

A trained neck works on that second problem. Stronger, well-conditioned neck muscles help an athlete brace before contact and slow how quickly the head accelerates after it. The evidence here is honest but imperfect. Most of it is associational, like the Collins high school study that linked greater neck strength to lower concussion odds. It doesn't prove cause and effect, so I don't oversell it.

What makes it an easy call is the cost. Neck work takes ten to fifteen minutes, two or three times a week: isometric holds in all four directions, banded or plate-loaded flexion and extension, and shrugs for the upper traps. Progress slowly.

Short version: wear the cap and train the neck. They aren't competing for the same job.
