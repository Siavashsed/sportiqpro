**Title:** Anyone else stopped trusting tested 1RM percentages and started letting bar speed decide when to end a set?

I coach strength and conditioning, and a morning a couple of years ago changed how I program. I had a pro hockey winger squatting at 85% of a 1RM we'd tested three weeks earlier. Six-forty in the morning, and the bar was crawling. On paper he was fine. In reality he was clearly under-recovered, and the percentage had no way of knowing that.

That's the core problem with fixed percentages: they assume the athlete you tested weeks ago is the athlete standing under the bar today. Sleep, travel, game load and stress all move that number.

Velocity-based training flips it. You track bar speed with a device, and the speed tells you the truth. If the bar is slower than usual at a given weight, you're fatigued. If it's faster, you've got room to push. You can also end a set when velocity drops a set amount (say 20%) from your best rep, instead of counting to a predetermined number.

It isn't perfect. Devices vary in accuracy and you need a reliable baseline for each lift. But for deciding when to back off, it's been far more honest than my old spreadsheets.

Curious whether anyone here uses velocity cutoffs, and what thresholds have worked for you.

## QUORA
**Answer this question:** What is velocity-based training and how does it help you autoregulate fatigue?

Velocity-based training (VBT) is a method where you measure how fast the bar moves on each rep and use that speed, rather than a fixed percentage of your one-rep max, to decide the load and when to stop a set.

The logic is simple. For any given weight, your bar speed on a fresh day is fairly consistent. If the bar is slower than usual at that weight, you're fatigued. If it's faster, you're primed and can push harder. So the bar gives you a real-time readiness check that a number from a testing day weeks ago can't.

Here's why that matters. A 1RM is a snapshot. Sleep, stress, travel, competition load and training history all shift what you can actually do on a given morning. I learned this working with a pro hockey winger who was squatting at 85% of a max we'd tested three weeks earlier. On paper the load was fine. The bar was visibly slow, and he was running on empty.

In practice, using VBT looks like this:

1. Establish a baseline. Record your bar speed at several loads when you're fresh, so you know what normal looks like for each lift.
2. Set a target speed range for the day's goal, such as strength, power or speed-strength.
3. Adjust load up or down if your first reps fall outside that range.
4. End the set when velocity drops by a chosen percentage from your fastest rep, commonly somewhere around 10 to 20 percent depending on the goal.

A few caveats. Devices differ in accuracy, so consistency matters more than which brand you pick. Use the same device, the same technique and the same lift definitions. And VBT works best as a guide alongside coaching judgment, not a replacement for it.
