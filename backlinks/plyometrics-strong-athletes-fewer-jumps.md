**Title:** Do your stronger athletes actually need more plyo volume, or less?

I keep seeing the same thing with strong athletes. The stronger they get under the bar, the fewer jumps they seem to need. Most programs I see do the opposite and keep stacking more contacts on top.

Plenty of high school whiteboards still show 150-contact plyo circuits. That might be fine for a kid who can barely squat bodyweight. For an athlete who already squats heavy, I've had better results with roughly 40 to 80 quality foot contacts per session. I put them right next to the heavy lifts instead of tacking them on at the end, when everyone is cooked and the ground contacts get long and sloppy.

What changed my mind was a rugby forward in 2019. He squatted 210 kg and still didn't jump anywhere near what that number suggested. Adding more jumping didn't fix it. Fewer, sharper contacts placed around the heavy work did.

I'm curious how others here handle it. Do you cut plyo volume as max strength goes up, or keep it the same? Do you pair jumps with the main lift contrast-style, or keep them on separate days?

## QUORA
**Answer this question:** How many plyometric jumps per session should athletes who already lift heavy do?

Fewer than most programs give them, and placed more carefully.

I'm a strength and conditioning coach. The mistake I see most often is plyometric volume that never changes as an athlete gets stronger. A freshman who can barely squat bodyweight and a senior squatting double bodyweight both get the same 150-contact circuit. That doesn't make sense.

Here's how I think about it. A strong athlete already has a big force base. What they usually lack is the ability to produce that force quickly. That is a quality problem, not a quantity problem. Every contact should be fast, crisp and done fresh. Once ground contacts get long and sloppy, you are training fatigue, not power.

For athletes who already squat heavy, I generally work with 40 to 80 quality foot contacts per session. Less-trained athletes can handle more low-intensity volume and often benefit from it. The advanced lifter gets more from less.

Placement matters just as much as the number. I put jumps right next to the heavy work, either just before the main lift or paired with it. They don't get tacked on at the end of a long session when the athlete is already cooked.

A few rules I use:
- Count contacts, not sets.
- End the set when quality drops, even if you haven't reached the number.
- Cut volume as jump intensity rises. Depth jumps cost far more than pogo hops.
- Track jump height or contact time so you know it's working.

I learned this the hard way in 2019 with a rugby forward who squatted 210 kg and still didn't jump like it. I wrote up the full approach here if you want the details: https://siavashsed.github.io/sportiqpro/plyometrics-strong-athletes-fewer-jumps/

Start lower than you think, and let the quality of each contact tell you when to add more.
