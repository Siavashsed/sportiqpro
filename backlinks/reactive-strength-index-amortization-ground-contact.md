## QUORA
**Answer this question:** How do I interpret ground contact time in reactive strength index testing, and what does it actually tell me about an athlete's elastic qualities?

Ground contact time is one of the most misunderstood metrics in RSI testing, and it's often where coaches' assumptions get challenged.

Basically, ground contact time measures how long your athlete's foot is in contact with the force plate during a jump—from touchdown to takeoff. This duration directly reflects the amortization phase, which is the critical window between eccentric loading (sinking down) and concentric drive (jumping up).

Here's what it actually reveals: Athletes with shorter ground contact times relative to their jump height have better elastic recoil. They're loading efficiently and transferring that energy back into movement without wasting time. Longer ground contact times suggest the athlete is spending more time in the stretched position, which typically means they're relying more on conscious muscle effort than true elastic qualities.

The catch? Visual assessment is terrible at detecting this. An athlete might *look* explosive—great vertical, quick movements—but force plate data shows extended ground contact, meaning they're compensating with strength rather than elasticity. That's crucial information because it completely changes how you structure plyometric training.

I ran into this directly with academy sprinters. The athlete I thought had elite elastic properties ranked significantly lower than expected once we looked at the ground contact data. It forced me to rethink programming for that entire group.
