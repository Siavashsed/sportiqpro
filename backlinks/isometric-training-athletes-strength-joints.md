**Title:** Why do so few athletes do isometric work until they're already hurt?

Something I keep seeing with athletes who come to me: almost none of them have done real isometric work. They've done lots of squats, cleans and plyos, but almost no holds.

The athlete who changed my mind was a basketball guard with a cranky patellar tendon. Even bodyweight split squats hurt him. We anchored a cheap loop band to a rack and had him hold a fixed position instead of moving through the range. He did long holds, around 45 seconds, for a few sets. He could work hard without the pain flaring, and he wasn't wrecked the next day.

That's the part people underrate. Isometrics put real tension through a joint and tendon at a specific angle for very little fatigue. So they fit around practice and games instead of competing with them.

Here's how I use them now. In season I program mid-thigh pulls and wall sit variations. I use split squat holds for knees. And I use overcoming isos against pins when someone needs to learn what max intent feels like.

Do you program these regularly, or only bring them out for rehab?

## QUORA
**Answer this question:** Are isometric exercises actually effective for building strength in athletes?

Yes, and they are one of the most underused tools in athletic strength work. I'm a strength and conditioning coach, and most athletes who come to me have barely touched them.

In an isometric, the muscle produces force without the joint moving. Examples are holding a split squat at a set depth, pushing into pins that will not move, or pulling a fixed bar from mid-thigh. There is no lowering phase and very little movement. That means you can create high tension at a specific joint angle with far less soreness than heavy dynamic lifting.

That matters for athletes in three ways.

First, strength. In overcoming isometrics you push or pull against something immovable with maximal intent, which teaches an athlete to recruit hard. Gains are strongest near the angle you train, so pick angles that matter for the sport, like a quarter squat position for jumping.

Second, tendons and joints. Longer holds at moderate effort, around 30 to 45 seconds, are widely used for irritated tendons. I once coached a basketball guard whose patellar tendon hurt during bodyweight split squats. With a band anchored to a rack and sustained holds, he kept loading the knee without a flare-up.

Third, fatigue. In season, athletes have no recovery to spare. Isometrics give a strength stimulus that does not leave them sore for practice.

They do not fully replace dynamic lifting. Athletes still need speed, range of motion and strength through the lowering phase. But as a complement, especially in season or around a sore joint, they earn their place.

An easy way to start is 3 to 5 holds of 30 to 45 seconds, two or three times a week, at the angles where the athlete is weakest or sore.
