**Title:** Why did so many athletes come back from the 2020 lockdown fit but still get hurt when games restarted?

I've been thinking about this a lot since the 2020 restart. One of the footballers I train sent me a video in March of himself doing split squats with a 20 kilo sandbag in a garage at 6 a.m. Honestly, I thought he'd be fine. He'd kept moving, kept lifting what he had, and his conditioning numbers looked decent.

He wasn't, quite. When games came back he picked up soft tissue problems that none of his fitness tests predicted.

What I took from it is that the restart wasn't a fitness problem, it was a load management problem. Lots of staffs treated lockdown like a normal offseason, then expected athletes to handle a compressed fixture schedule. But an offseason still includes high-speed running, sport-specific change of direction and match intensity at some point. A garage doesn't give you that, and tissue tolerance for sprinting and repeated accelerations drops faster than aerobic fitness does.

The lesson I keep coming back to is that general fitness and match readiness are different things, and the ramp-up back to full load needs to be planned around exposure, not around how fit someone looks.

Curious whether any of you coached or trained through the restart and saw the same pattern, or whether I'm overreading a small sample.

## QUORA
**Answer this question:** Why did so many professional athletes get injured after sport restarted following the 2020 COVID lockdown?

I'm a strength and conditioning coach, and I worked with several footballers through that period, so this is what I saw first-hand.

The short version: the restart wasn't really a fitness problem. It was a load management problem.

Most athletes trained during lockdown. They ran, lifted what equipment they had, and did conditioning work. Many came back with decent aerobic numbers. But being fit and being ready for match load are different things.

Three factors stood out:

1. Missing high-speed exposure. Hamstrings, adductors and calves adapt to sprinting by sprinting. A garage, a park or a treadmill rarely gives repeated maximal velocity efforts, so tissue tolerance dropped even when fitness didn't.

2. Missing chaotic load. Matches involve sudden decelerations, cuts, contact and unpredictable movement. Solo training can't reproduce that.

3. Compressed schedules. Many leagues restarted with fewer rest days and condensed fixtures. That meant athletes went from low exposure to peak demand almost overnight, with little time for gradual adaptation.

The staffs who handled it best treated the return as a progressive build in exposure, not a switch flipped back on. They monitored high-speed running volume, rotated squads, and used friendlies as a proper ramp-up rather than a formality.

The staffs who struggled treated lockdown as an extended offseason, assumed fitness test results meant readiness, and went straight to competition rhythm.

If you coach or train athletes, the practical lesson applies far beyond 2020: any time someone has a long break, plan the return around exposure to the specific demands of the sport, and respect that tissues adapt more slowly than the lungs do.
