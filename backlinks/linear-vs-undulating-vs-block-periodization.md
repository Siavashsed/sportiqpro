**Title:** For lifters past their second year, does straight linear periodization still work for anyone?

This question is for coaches and lifters past the intermediate stage. How do you structure your training year now?

Here is how I read it afterward. All of them had more than two years of serious training behind them. One slow progression from volume to intensity didn't give them enough variety in the stimulus. They adapted to the early phase and then spent weeks on work that no longer moved them.

What has worked better for me since is a block structure with undulating days inside each block. The block sets the emphasis for the phase. Inside the week, heavy, moderate and light days rotate, so you never spend long at one rep range.

Linear still makes sense for beginners. For anyone past year two, I wouldn't go back to it.

Linear periodization isn't broken. It's built for beginners, who make progress from almost any steady increase in load. Trained athletes adapt faster, and one long slide from volume to intensity leaves them stuck on a stimulus they stopped responding to weeks earlier.

The structure I use now is block periodization with undulating days inside it. Each block has a clear emphasis. Inside the week, heavy, moderate and light sessions rotate, so the athlete keeps getting a new signal without losing direction.

The model you choose should match the athlete's training age, not the template you learned first.

## QUORA
**Answer this question:** Is linear periodization still effective for intermediate and advanced lifters?

In my experience, mostly no. Linear periodization is very good for beginners. For anyone with more than about two years of serious training, it's usually the weakest of the three main models.

Linear periodization moves through one long sequence. You start with high volume and moderate loads, then cut the volume and raise the intensity week by week. A beginner responds to almost any steady increase in load, so this works well for them. A trained lifter adapts much faster. Once they've adapted to the current phase, they can spend weeks on work that no longer drives progress.

I saw this firsthand with a group of 14 academy rugby forwards. I put them through a classic 12-week linear plan, starting at 4x10 and ending at 5x3. By week nine, half of them had stalled on the back squat. These weren't new lifters, and the plan simply didn't give them enough variety in the stimulus.

The two alternatives:

Undulating periodization (DUP) changes the rep ranges and intensity within the week, for example a heavy day, a moderate day and a light day. The athlete gets a frequent new signal and practices heavy loads often.

Block periodization splits the training year into focused phases, typically accumulation, transmutation and realization. Each block concentrates on one quality before moving to the next.

What I program now for trained athletes is a hybrid: block structure on the macro level, with undulating days inside each block. The block gives the season direction, and the daily variation keeps adaptation going.

I wrote up the full comparison of all three models, including how I'd choose between them, here: https://siavashsed.github.io/sportiqpro/linear-vs-undulating-vs-block-periodization/

The short answer: keep linear for novices. Past year two, use blocks with undulating days.
