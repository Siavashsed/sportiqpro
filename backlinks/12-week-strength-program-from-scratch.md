**Title:** Anyone else find that cutting exercises from a 12-week block made their lifters stronger, not weaker?

Coached a collegiate rugby squad in Leeds back in 2019 and made the classic mistake: handed everyone a 22-exercise template at 6 a.m. in a freezing weight room. By week five, half the lads couldn't tell me what they'd squatted the previous week. Honestly, neither could I.

The problem wasn't effort. It was that nobody could track progress across that many movements, so nobody could tell if the program was working. Variety was drowning out the signal.

What I do now is three 4-week blocks built around five lifts that don't get swapped out: a squat pattern, a hinge, a horizontal press, a vertical pull, and one loaded carry or overhead press depending on the athlete. Accessories rotate between blocks, the main lifts stay put. Week 12 numbers are directly comparable to week 1, which makes the whole thing far easier to coach and far easier for lifters to stay bought into.

The lifters who complained about boredom in week three were usually the ones hitting PRs in week ten.

## QUORA
**Answer this question:** How should I structure a 12-week strength training program from scratch?

Keep it simpler than you think. The most common mistake I see, and one I made myself, is building a program with too many exercises. Early in my coaching career I gave a collegiate rugby squad a 22-exercise template. By week five nobody, including me, could say what they'd lifted the week before. The program wasn't hard; it was untrackable.

Here's the structure I use now:

1. Split the 12 weeks into three 4-week blocks. Each block has a clear job: build a base and learn the movements, add load, then push intensity and test.

2. Pick five lifts and keep them for all 12 weeks. A typical set is a squat variation, a hinge such as a deadlift or Romanian deadlift, a horizontal press, a vertical or horizontal pull, and an overhead press or loaded carry. Don't swap these. Progress only shows up when you repeat the same movement and watch the numbers move.

3. Rotate accessories between blocks, not within them. Two or three accessory movements per session is plenty. Changing them every four weeks gives you variety without wrecking your ability to compare week to week.

4. Log everything. Weight, reps, and how hard it felt. If you can't look back at week two while you're in week nine, you're guessing.

5. Plan a lighter week at the end of each block so fatigue doesn't pile up unnoticed.

Training three days a week is enough for most people starting out. You'll feel bored around week three. That's normal, and it usually passes right before the numbers start climbing.
