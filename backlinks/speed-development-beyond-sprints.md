**Title:** Sprinting more is not always the fix for a speed plateau. How do you find the real limiter?

Something I keep seeing with field sport athletes: when speed stalls, the default fix is more sprinting. Sprinting matters. It sharpens mechanics and gets the body used to high velocity. But it won't fix a force deficit, an ankle that collapses on ground contact, or a hamstring that can't tolerate the last 20 meters of a rep.

Back in 2019 I coached a 21-year-old winger whose flying 10 sat at 1.04 seconds on Freelap for eight straight weeks. He was sprinting three times a week. More exposure wasn't moving the number. We cut him to two sprint sessions and put that time into heavy strength work, and that's when things started to change.

These are the questions I ask now before adding sprint volume:
- Can they produce enough force relative to body weight?
- Is the ankle stiff enough to use ground contact instead of absorbing it?
- Do the hamstrings hold up at top speed, or do reps fade late?

How do others here diagnose a speed plateau? Do you test strength qualities first or adjust the sprint programming?

## QUORA
**Answer this question:** Why isn't my athlete getting faster even though they sprint several times a week?

I'm a strength and conditioning coach, and this is one of the most common plateaus I see. The short answer is that sprinting is necessary for speed, but once an athlete has a base of sprint exposure it is rarely the whole answer.

Sprinting does two things well. It refines mechanics, and it gets the nervous system and tissues comfortable at high velocity. What it does not do well is fix the physical qualities that cap top speed. Three limiters come up again and again:

1. A force deficit. If an athlete cannot push enough force into the ground relative to body weight, extra reps at the same output just rehearse the same speed.

2. A soft ankle. Ground contacts at top speed are very short. An ankle and foot that give way on contact absorb energy instead of returning it.

3. Hamstrings that cannot tolerate the late phase of a rep. If reps fade over the last 20 meters, or the athlete feels tight there, the hamstrings are probably the limiter. That is also an injury risk.

Here is a real example. In 2019 I coached a 21-year-old winger whose flying 10 held at 1.04 seconds for eight straight weeks while he sprinted three times a week. We cut him to two sprint sessions and used the freed time for heavy strength work. That shift got him moving again, not extra volume. I wrote up the full approach here if you want the detail: https://siavashsed.github.io/sportiqpro/speed-development-beyond-sprints/

In your position I would keep sprinting but test before adding volume. Check relative strength, film ground contacts, and watch whether times drop off late in reps. Then train the weakest link, and speed usually follows.
